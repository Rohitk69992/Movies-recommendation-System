# Comprehensive Viva Voce Questions & Answers

**Project**: CineMatch — TMDB Content-Based Movie Recommendation System  
**Audience**: External Examiners, Faculty Evaluators, and Viva Panels  
**Subject**: Machine Learning / Recommender Systems  

---

### Q1: What is a recommendation system?
**Answer**:  
A recommendation system is an information filtering engine that predicts the interest, preference, or rating a user would assign to an unseen item. Its primary purpose is to alleviate information overload by ranking and presenting a personalized subset of items from a large catalog that best match user intent or thematic context.

---

### Q2: What is content-based filtering?
**Answer**:  
Content-based filtering recommends items based on the **intrinsic characteristics and attributes** of the items themselves. In movie recommendation, content consists of textual and categorical descriptors such as genres, narrative synopsis, lead actors, director, and plot keywords. An item profile is created by vectorizing these descriptors, and recommendations are generated by measuring the geometric or semantic proximity between a reference item's vector and candidate item vectors in the catalog.

---

### Q3: Why is this project content-based filtering and not collaborative filtering?
**Answer**:  
- **Data Dependency**: CineMatch operates exclusively on movie metadata from TMDB (`overview`, `genres`, `keywords`, `cast`, `crew`). It requires **zero user interaction logs** (no user IDs, ratings, clickstreams, or watch history).
- **Recommendation Paradigm**: Collaborative filtering models user-item utility matrices ($\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{P} \mathbf{Q}^T$) to identify correlations between communities of users with similar historical behavior. Content-based filtering calculates item-to-item geometric similarity in a fixed feature space.
- **Cold-Start**: Unlike collaborative filtering, which suffers from user and item cold-start, CineMatch can immediately recommend a brand-new movie the moment its metadata is added to the catalog, without needing anyone to watch or rate it first.

---

### Q4: What metadata features were selected and why?
**Answer**:  
1. **Overview**: Free-text narrative summary providing thematic context and plot motifs.
2. **Genres**: Broad story classifications (e.g. Science Fiction, Action, Thriller).
3. **Keywords**: Specific thematic descriptors (e.g. superhero, artificial intelligence, time travel).
4. **Cast (Top 3)**: The leading actors (billing order 0, 1, 2) who dictate star appeal and acting style.
5. **Director (Crew where `job == 'Director'`)**: The creative auteur shaping visual tone, pacing, and storytelling.

*Excluded Features*: Budget, revenue, vote average, and popularity were intentionally excluded from the vectorizer to prevent financial and popularity bias from distorting content-based similarity.

---

### Q5: Why is text vectorization required in this system?
**Answer**:  
Mathematical algorithms and geometric distance metrics (such as cosine similarity or Euclidean distance) cannot operate directly on raw strings or textual documents. Vectorization maps qualitative textual tokens into numerical vector representations in a multi-dimensional Euclidean vector space $\mathbb{R}^V$, enabling linear algebraic operations such as dot products and norm calculations.

---

### Q6: Why does the reference project use CountVectorizer instead of TF-IDF?
**Answer**:  
- **Methodological Fidelity**: In the CampusX reference formulation, `CountVectorizer(max_features=5000, stop_words='english')` was chosen for pedagogical clarity and balanced term representation.
- **Behavioral Distinction**: `CountVectorizer` counts unigram frequencies. TF-IDF downweights words that appear across many documents (via $\log(N/df)$). In film metadata, genre terms (such as "Action" or "Drama") and recurring themes appear frequently across the catalog. TF-IDF can penalize shared genres because of their high document frequency, whereas in a metadata-driven recommender, shared genres are strong positive signals of similarity that should not be heavily discounted.

---

### Q7: How does cosine similarity work mathematically?
**Answer**:  
Cosine similarity measures the cosine of the angle between two multi-dimensional vectors in an inner product space:
$$\cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^V u_k v_k}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^V u_k^2} \sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^V v_k^2}}$$

- **Angle vs Magnitude**: It evaluates directional alignment rather than Euclidean magnitude. This means a movie with a 150-word synopsis and a movie with a 40-word synopsis that share identical proportions of terms will have a cosine similarity of $1.0$, whereas Euclidean distance would penalize them for length difference.
- **Range**: Since Bag-of-Words term frequencies are non-negative ($u_k \ge 0$), $\cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})$ is bounded in $[0.0, 1.0]$.

---

### Q8: What does the main diagonal of the similarity matrix represent?
**Answer**:  
The main diagonal entry $S(i, i)$ represents the similarity of movie $i$ with itself. Because any non-zero vector $\mathbf{v}_i$ is perfectly aligned with itself ($\theta = 0^\circ$), the diagonal value is always:
$$S(i, i) = \frac{\mathbf{v}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_i}{\|\mathbf{v}_i\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}_i\|_2} = 1.0$$

---

### Q9: Why must the selected query movie be excluded from the recommendations?
**Answer**:  
Because $S(q, q) = 1.0$ is the theoretical maximum similarity score, sorting the similarity vector descending without exclusion will always place the query movie at rank 1. Recommending the exact same movie a user just requested provides zero discovery utility. In CineMatch, the query movie index is explicitly masked ($S(q, q) \leftarrow -1.0$) prior to top-$K$ selection.

---

### Q10: How are the top-K recommendations selected and ranked?
**Answer**:  
1. Compute the similarity vector $\mathbf{s} = \text{cosine\_similarity}(\mathbf{v}_q, \mathbf{X})$.
2. Set $\mathbf{s}[q] = -1.0$ (self-exclusion).
3. Identify top $K + 10$ candidates using $O(N)$ partial sorting (`np.argpartition`).
4. Apply deterministic tie-breaking using lexicographical sorting on $(-s_j, -\text{vote\_average}_j)$.
5. Slice the top $K$ items, retrieve metadata from the columnar Parquet store, and compute shared explainability tags.

---

### Q11: What happens if metadata is missing (e.g. missing overview or empty cast)?
**Answer**:  
- In preprocessing, missing overviews are handled by dropping the 3 affected records (CampusX style) or imputing empty strings.
- Empty JSON lists (`[]`) for cast, crew, or keywords are handled safely by `safe_literal_eval()`, yielding empty lists `[]`.
- If a movie has zero keywords or cast, its vector will still encode its genres and synopsis tokens. The algorithm does not crash and computes similarity based on available features.

---

### Q12: What happens if two movies share the same title?
**Answer**:  
In the TMDB dataset, three title pairs are homonyms: *The Host* (2006 vs 2013), *Batman* (1989 vs 1966), and *Out of the Blue* (1980 vs 2006).
- **The Defect**: Joining tables on `title` produces a Cartesian product ($2 \times 2 = 4$ rows per pair), pairing Tim Burton's Batman with 1966 cast/crew.
- **The Solution**: CineMatch joins tables strictly on primary key `id == movie_id`. In the UI, titles are displayed alongside their release year (e.g. *Batman (1989)* vs. *Batman (1966)*) to provide unambiguous resolution.

---

### Q13: What is the computational and memory complexity of a dense similarity matrix?
**Answer**:  
- **Storage Complexity**: Storing an $N \times N$ dense pairwise matrix scales quadratically as $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$. For $N = 4,800$, a float64 matrix requires:
  $$4800 \times 4800 \times 8 \text{ bytes} \approx 184.32 \text{ MB}$$
  For a commercial catalog of $100,000$ movies, this would require $\approx 80 \text{ GB}$ of RAM!
- **Engineering Optimization**: CineMatch avoids storing the dense matrix. It retains the sparse term matrix ($N \times V$, $\approx 295 \text{ KB}$ on disk) and computes single-row dot products on the fly in $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot \text{nnz})$ time, taking just **7 milliseconds** per query.

---

### Q14: What are the fundamental limitations of this content-based approach?
**Answer**:  
1. **Lack of Serendipity / Filter Bubble**: Only recommends items similar to what the user chose. A user who likes Sci-Fi will never discover an outstanding Documentary.
2. **Quality Agnosticism**: Two movies with identical synopsis terms will score high similarity, even if one is an award-winning classic and the other is poorly produced.
3. **Absence of User History**: Cannot personalize recommendations based on individual taste profiles, watch durations, or past ratings.
4. **Item Cold-Start on Sparse Metadata**: Obscure films with short synopses have sparse vectors and rarely get recommended.

---

### Q15: How could relevance be evaluated if ground-truth user labels were available?
**Answer**:  
If historical user interaction or rating logs were collected:
1. **Precision@K**: The proportion of the top-$K$ recommended movies that the user actually watched or rated $\ge 4$ stars.
2. **Recall@K**: The proportion of all movies the user liked that appeared in the top-$K$ list.
3. **NDCG@K (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain)**: Measures ranking quality by penalizing relevant items appearing lower in the list.
4. **Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)**: Evaluates how high the first relevant movie appears.

### Q16: What architectural changes would be required to convert this into a hybrid recommender?
**Answer**:  
1. **User Interaction Log**: Ingest a user-item rating or interaction matrix (e.g. MovieLens or clickstream).
2. **Collaborative Model**: Train a matrix factorization model (e.g. SVD, ALS, or Neural Collaborative Filtering) to produce latent user embeddings $\mathbf{p}_u$ and item embeddings $\mathbf{q}_i$.
3. **Hybrid Blending**:
   $$\text{Score}_{\text{hybrid}}(u, i) = \alpha \cdot \text{Score}_{\text{content}}(m_q, i) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \hat{r}_{u, i}$$
   or train a learning-to-rank model (e.g. XGBoost or LightGBM) using both content similarity and collaborative scores as input features to generate the final top-$K$ ranking.
